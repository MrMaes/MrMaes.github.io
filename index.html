From Digital Artifacts to a Blended Course I Could Actually Teach

By John Maes September 2026 6-minute read

The hardest part of blended-course design was not finding technology. It was turning a semester of tools, theories, and individual assignments into one course in which every choice served the learners and the mathematics.

Over the course of developing my blended curriculum, I moved from a theoretical framework and separate digital learning centers to an ASSURE unit, a flipped lesson, a digital storytelling assessment, and a final collaboration cycle. My blended Algebra 1 unit, Making Decisions with Linear Models, asked students to move among tables, graphs, equations, and real-world contexts as they interpreted linear relationships and solved systems of equations. Related AP Statistics learning centers asked students to identify misleading representations, distinguish association from causation, and judge whether a model was reasonable in context. The challenge was making these pieces feel like parts of the same instructional philosophy rather than a collection of completed assignments.

The challenge of assigning every tool a real job

It was easy to imagine a long list of platforms. It was much harder to determine why each one belonged. I used Padlet for collaboration, Desmos for modeling and graphing, DeltaMath for focused practice and feedback, Canvas for organization and reflection, and Google Forms for individual checks. The AP Statistics learning centers followed the same pattern: move students from preparation to investigation and explanation. I still had to ask whether each digital activity improved the learning task or only changed its location.

Padlet, for example, gave students a place to post strategies, compare representations, and revise explanations after seeing the thinking of others. Its value was not that students were posting online. Its value was that their reasoning became visible enough to discuss, assess, and revise. Desmos was useful when it allowed students to test and connect representations quickly, but a graphing tool could not replace the need to explain why the intersection of two lines was the turning point in a practical story. DeltaMath could provide timely feedback, but it also carried the risk of turning a reasoning-centered lesson into isolated screen-based practice.

Access to a device is not the same thing as access to the learning. The task, the scaffolds, and the feedback still determine who can participate meaningfully.

Designing for access without lowering the thinking

A second challenge was planning for multilingual students and students who benefit from support with attention, organization, or executive functioning. Blended learning can create flexibility, but it can also multiply directions, tabs, logins, and opportunities to become lost. Sentence frames, visual models, short chunks, predictable routines, and clear transitions therefore had to be part of the original design.

Uneven Wi-Fi and device access required the same attention. My best response was a hybrid approach: district devices for consistency and security, teacher-authorized phones only for a specific purpose, and equivalent non-phone or offline options. Equity is not solved by putting a device in every student’s hand; it requires multiple legitimate ways into the same rigorous task.

Keeping collaboration from hiding misconceptions

Collaboration was one of the strongest parts of the unit, but a polished group post can hide individual misconceptions. Structured roles and partner talk helped, yet they were not enough by themselves. Next time, every collaboration cycle would include a private prediction, a group discussion and post, and a short individual explanation or verification. I would also model the routine more explicitly and collect student feedback earlier. That sequence would preserve the value of shared thinking while giving me better evidence of who could transfer the reasoning independently.

What I would do differently next time

My next version would begin with a tighter planning question: What should students be able to see, say, test, or revise here that would be difficult without this tool? If I could not answer that question clearly, I would remove the tool. I would also prototype one complete learning cycle before building all three lessons. Testing a short sequence—launch, individual attempt, collaboration, feedback, revision, and individual check—would reveal navigation and timing problems before they were repeated across the unit.

I would also make the flipped portion more deliberate. Moving first exposure outside class is useful only when it protects class time for clarification, coaching, critique, and decision-making. The video would remain short and captioned, with a transcript, guided notes, an offline copy, and a no-penalty catch-up option. Most importantly, I would protect more time for listening to explanations and asking follow-up questions. Technology can organize participation and feedback, but it cannot decide which student idea deserves another question.

If the learners were fellow educators

If this course were professional development for peer educators, I would not simply replace the Algebra 1 examples with adult-facing slides. Teachers bring experience, limited time, and immediate classroom problems. I would therefore use a design studio rather than a sequence of tutorials. Each participant would bring one lesson, identify the instructional job each tool should perform, and redesign one complete learning cycle. Padlet could hold lesson artifacts, implementation questions, and peer feedback. As I concluded when writing about blogs and other Web 2.0 tools, educators should not only consume information; they should create, respond, and learn from one another’s practice.

The format would model the same choices I was asking teachers to make: short asynchronous preparation, an efficient workshop, protected design time, and structured peer response. Teachers would leave with a usable lesson, an accessibility check, and a plan for collecting evidence. In a BYOD setting, I would model district Chromebooks for equity and security, smartphones only for a limited teacher-authorized task, and an equivalent non-phone option. Participants would later return with student work and revise together.

Most importantly, I would avoid presenting a list of “best” tools. Tools change, district policies differ, and another login is rarely what teachers need most. The durable learning is a way of thinking: begin with the goal, select technology for a specific instructional job, provide an equivalent path for access, and look for evidence that students are reasoning—not merely clicking. The blog could then become a continuing professional space: a record of what teachers tried, what the evidence showed, and what they would revise. Building this course reminded me that good blended learning is still good teaching. Technology matters most when it strengthens that fact.
