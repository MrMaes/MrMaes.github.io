About John

Mathematics teacher · doctoral student · persistent reviser

John Maes

I teach mathematics by making thinking visible.

I am a high school mathematics teacher in Virginia, where I teach Algebra 1 and AP Statistics. My classrooms include students with a wide range of mathematical backgrounds, language experiences, and learning needs. That variety has shaped the way I think about instruction. I want students to have multiple ways to enter a problem, but I also want every student to encounter meaningful mathematical reasoning.

I am currently completing an Ed.D. in Curriculum and Instruction. My doctoral work has deepened my interest in how teachers support students’ written statistical reasoning and how instructional design can make complex thinking more accessible without reducing its rigor. I am also pursuing National Board Certification in adolescent and young-adult mathematics and continuing to grow as an AP Statistics educator.

Why this blog exists

Visible Reasoning is a space to share practical ideas, honest reflections, and lessons learned from the classroom. The purpose is not to promote technology for its own sake. I am interested in when a digital tool genuinely improves access, feedback, collaboration, or transfer—and when a simpler approach would serve students better. I plan to use this blog to examine blended learning, mathematical communication, assessment, and the small design decisions that often determine whether students can participate fully.

Educators improve when we can see one another’s unfinished thinking as well as the final product. For that reason, this blog will include approaches that worked, challenges that remain unresolved, and revisions I would make the next time. My hope is that other teachers can take an idea, adapt it to their own students, and contribute their own experience to the conversation.

Beyond the classroom

Outside of teaching and doctoral work, I enjoy rock climbing, running, and motorcycles. I like activities that reward patience, careful attention, and incremental improvement. Those interests are different from teaching on the surface, but they share something I value: progress usually comes from noticing what happened, making one thoughtful adjustment, and trying again.

Professional goals

My professional goals are to strengthen the quality of mathematical reasoning in my classroom, complete my doctorate and National Board Certification, and continue growing as a teacher-leader. I want to help develop curriculum and professional learning that teachers can actually use—work that respects their expertise, addresses the realities of diverse classrooms, and keeps evidence of student learning at the center. Over time, I also hope to expand my work in advanced statistics and dual-enrollment mathematics while continuing to learn from the students and educators around me.
